CISCaRL · a guided walkthrough

From decision trees to personalized treatment effects

This guide assumes you just learned decision trees and random forests — and nothing else. We build every new idea (survival data, causal effects, pseudo-outcomes, stability selection, conformal intervals) from scratch, using one tiny dataset you can check by hand.

Start here

What you already know, and what we're about to add

You've trained a model that takes features X and predicts a number y. A random forest does this by averaging many decision trees; each tree recursively splits the data at feature thresholds. Everything below builds on that picture — no exotic math required.

Here is the entire journey in one line, then we unpack each box:

1Make a numeric target
Turn survival data into one number per patient to regress on.
2Grow trees on it
Exactly the random forests you know.
3Harvest rules
Every root-to-leaf path is a patient subgroup.
4Vote on rules
Bootstrap many times; keep the rules that keep winning.
5Add error bars
Give every kept rule a 90% interval it can back up.

Section 1

The question: does this treatment help this patient?

Imagine a drug. On average it helps a little. But averages hide the interesting part:

  • Some patients get a large benefit.
  • Some get nothing (taking a drug for no reason still costs money and side effects).
  • Some are harmed.

A model that just says "the drug helps by +5%" is not useful to a doctor deciding about one specific person. We want the personal effect. Formally, for patient features x and a time horizon t*:

τ(x) = P(alive at t* if given the drug) − P(alive at t* if not given the drug)

This quantity τ(x) is the CATE. If τ(x) = +0.15, the drug raises this patient's chance of surviving past t* by 15 percentage points. If τ(x) = −0.08, the drug is likely hurting them. That negative case is the whole reason personalization matters.

Pause & check. Studying two extra hours raises the class average by +5 marks. Is that a CATE?
No — it's an average treatment effect. The CATE would be the +12 for struggling students and the +0 for top students. CISCaRL's job is to discover those subgroups automatically.
Section 2 · new concept

Problem 1: patients disappear from the study (censoring)

In a normal ML dataset every row has a label y. Medical studies are different. We follow each patient for a while and record:

  • T — how many months we observed them (time until the event, or until we lost them).
  • Δ (delta) — 1 if we saw the actual event (e.g. death), 0 if they were censored (moved away, study ended, withdrew).

Censoring is the missing-label problem of survival analysis. A patient censored at month 5 might still be alive at month 12 — we simply don't know. Throwing them away wastes data; pretending they died biases everything.

new concept

Fix in one sentence

Pick a horizon t* ("alive at 12 months?"). For each patient, ask: is their status at t* knowable?

SituationStatus at t*Keep for training?
T > t* (observed past t*)Alive → Y = 1Yes
T ≤ t* and Δ = 1 (saw death early)Dead → Y = 0Yes
T ≤ t* and Δ = 0 (lost before t*)UnknownNo — drop

The dropped patients aren't ignored for free, though: survivors who resemble the dropouts are up-weighted to compensate. That re-weighting is called IPCW (Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting), and we'll see it used twice: once for training, once for the error bars.

Pause & check. Patient A: T = 20, Δ = 0. Patient B: T = 4, Δ = 1. With t* = 12, who is "known" and what is each Y?
A: T > 12, so known, and since they were observed alive past 12, Y = 1 (the fact they were later censored at 20 doesn't matter). B: died at 4 before the horizon, known, Y = 0.

Section 4 · new concept

Why we turn "survival" into a yes/no question

Survival data naturally lives on a time axis. You could model the whole curve, but the research question here is simpler: does the treatment change the chance of being alive at a fixed, clinically meaningful horizon? (e.g. 12 months).

Fix t*. The outcome collapses to a single binary number per patient:

Y = 1 if alive at t*,  Y = 0 if dead by t*,  Y = ? if censored before t*

Benefits of this choice for a beginner:

  • It's just a label. No need to model hazard functions or survival curves to follow the algorithm.
  • It matches clinical decisions. "Will this patient be alive next year?" is actionable.
  • It plugs into ordinary ML. The target of step 6 will be a single number per patient, so anything that fits numeric targets (including random forests) applies.

The cost: you must choose t*. In the project it defaults to the median observed event time, and the same pipeline reruns for different horizons.

Section 5 · new concept

Problem 2: we never see both worlds

Here is the fundamental obstacle of causal inference. To know a drug's true effect on patient 1 we would need to observe patient 1 twice: once with the drug and once without. Reality gives us only one of those. The counterfactual ("what would have happened otherwise") is permanently hidden.

So we cannot simply compute Y(treated) − Y(untreated) per person. What can we do? Compare groups — but only if the groups are comparable.

The naive comparison and why it lies

Suppose we just average Y for treated patients minus average Y for control patients:

naive effect = mean(Y | Z=1) − mean(Y | Z=0)

If doctors tend to give the drug to sicker patients, the treated group is different from the start. The gap then mixes up two things: the drug effect and the type of patient selected. This is confounding.

new concept

Propensity score: how likely were you to get the drug?

Fit a small classifier predicting treatment from features: e(x) = P(Z = 1 | X = x). If the treated and control groups have the same e(x) distribution, they're comparable in the features that drive treatment. Two flavors of dataset:

  • Randomized trial (RCT): coin-flip assignment, so e(x) = 0.5 for everyone. Groups are comparable by design.
  • Observational data: e(x) varies. We must correct.
Pause & check. In the toy table, is treatment assignment random? Compute mean(Z).
Z = 1 for ids 1,3,5,7,9,10 → 6 out of 12 = 0.5. Also note young patients (cd4 high) tend to be treated. It's a balanced-ish but not guaranteed-random set; either way the machinery below handles it.
Section 6 · new concept

The key trick: invent a numeric target per patient

We can't observe individual effects, but we can construct a per-patient number whose average, across patients with the same features, equals the true effect. That number is called a pseudo-outcome (or pseudo-ITE). Once we have it, the causal problem becomes a plain supervised regression problem.

The DR-learner pseudo-outcome, term by term

Γᵢ = (Zᵢ − e(xᵢ)) / (e(xᵢ)(1 − e(xᵢ))) · (Yᵢ − μ_Z(xᵢ))  +  (μ₁(xᵢ) − μ₀(xᵢ))
PieceMeaningWhere it comes from
e(x)probability of being treatedlogistic regression on Z | X
μ₁(x)predicted survival chance if treatedfit on treated patients
μ₀(x)predicted survival chance if untreatedfit on control patients
Y − μ_Z(x)surprise: how much better/worse than predictedobserved label
(Z−e)/(e(1−e))weight fixing the confoundingpropensity
μ₁−μ₀baseline model's guess of the effectoutcome models

Read it as: "Start from the model's estimate of the effect (μ₁−μ₀). Then correct it using the patient's actual surprise, scaled so the correction averages out under confounding."

Worked example: patient 1

Patient 1 is treated (Z = 1), survived (Y = 1). Suppose e = 0.5, μ₁ = 0.70, μ₀ = 0.45.

surprise   = Y − μ₁         = 1 − 0.70 = 0.30
weight     = (1 − 0.5)/(0.5·0.5) = 2.0
correction = 2.0 × 0.30     = 0.60
baseline   = μ₁ − μ₀        = 0.25
Γ₁         = 0.60 + 0.25    = 0.85

Interpretation: the model expected a modest +0.25 effect, but this patient did even better than the treated prediction, so we nudge their effect estimate up to 0.85. Patients who do worse than expected get pushed down, potentially below zero.

Why "doubly robust"? The estimator is correct if either the propensity model or the outcome models are right — not necessarily both. That safety net is why DR-learners are popular. In an RCT where e = 0.5 is known, the propensity half is automatically correct.
Pause & check. A control patient has Z = 0, Y = 0, e = 0.5, μ₁ = 0.6, μ₀ = 0.4. Compute Γ.
surprise = 0 − 0.4 = −0.4; weight = (0 − 0.5)/0.25 = −2.0; correction = (−2.0)(−0.4) = +0.8; baseline = 0.2; Γ = 1.0. So even though this patient died, they died "worse than expected," implying the untreated outcome is low and the drug would have helped — a large positive pseudo-effect. This is exactly the kind of extrapolation the DR formula performs.

Censored-before-t* patients get Γ = NaN and are excluded from the regression. In code: python/utils.py::compute_pseudo_ite_dr.

Section 7 · you know this

Stage 1: now it's just supervised learning — grow trees on Γ

We now have a numeric target Γ for the 9 known patients. This is the moment the problem becomes familiar: predict Γ from features X. Use the tools you already know.

  • Random forest on Γ (RandomForestRegressor). Why a forest? Averaging many trees reduces the variance caused by the noisy pseudo-outcome. Each tree recursively splits patients at thresholds like cd4 ≤ 300 or age > 50. Because the splits are chosen to explain effect heterogeneity, the forest's structure already encodes which patient types have different effects.
  • Gradient boosting on the forest's prediction (GradientBoostingRegressor). A second, complementary set of splits fit to the smoothed surface. Two tree families = a richer menu of candidate subgroups.

Neither model is the final output. They are candidate generators — they hand us a large collection of plausible subgroups, and later stages decide which few are worth keeping.

you know this

Recall how a random forest trains

Each tree is fit on a bootstrap sample (rows drawn with replacement) and considers a random subset of features at each split. The split chosen minimizes the variance of Γ within the resulting two groups. You've seen all of this; the only change is that our y is now a pseudo-effect instead of a raw label.

Pause & check. The forest predicts Γ, which is noisy. Could you just call forest.predict(x) the CATE and stop?
You could — it's the black-box CSF baseline! It's often accurate but uninterpretable. CISCaRL's whole point is to turn this same signal into a short rule list. Keep this in mind: the forest is a stepping stone, not the answer.
Section 8 · new concept

From tree paths to human-readable rules

A decision tree assigns every patient to a leaf by following a path of yes/no questions. That path is a rule describing a subgroup. This is the bridge from "model" to "explanation."

A tree trained on Γ (illustrative):

  node 0: cd4 ≤ 300 ?
   ├── yes → node 1: age > 50 ?
   │          ├── yes → LEAF A  (ids 1,2,3,4,10,11)
   │          └── no  → LEAF B  (older-but-low-cd4 edge cases)
   └── no  → LEAF C  (ids 5,6,12: young, high cd4)

Walk from the root and record the conditions. The path to Leaf A becomes the rule:

IF cd4 ≤ 300 AND age > 50  →  subgroup {1,2,3,4,10,11}

Do this for every node of every tree in both forests. A 200-tree forest with depth ~10 yields thousands of candidate rules. In code: python/cis_carl.py::_extract_rules_from_tree and _extract_rules_from_ensemble. Each candidate is stored as a list of conditions like [(cd4, '<=', 300), (age, '>', 50)] plus a boolean mask marking which patients satisfy it.

Turning rules into a design matrix

Number the candidates j = 1…M. Build a matrix R with one column per rule:

R[i, j] = 1 if patient i satisfies rule j, else 0

        rule1(young)  rule2(old+lowcd4)  rule3(...)
 id 1        0              1               ...
 id 2        0              1
 id 5        1              0

Now "which subgroups matter?" becomes a standard variable selection problem: fit Γ ≈ R·w and find which columns get nonzero weight w. Because each column is a subgroup, a sparse weight vector is a short rule list. In code: _build_rule_matrix.

Deduplicate and cap. The same rule reappears across many trees. Deduplicate by its text form, and if there are more than max_rules = 2000, keep the ones with the highest within-rule variance of the target (those are the subgroups where the effect actually differs, i.e. informative).
Section 9 · new concept

Stage 2: why can't we just run one Lasso?

We have Γ and the rule matrix R. The obvious move is Lasso regression: it shrinks useless coefficients to exactly zero, giving a sparse set of rules. So why isn't that the end of the story?

Regression with Lasso, in two lines

Lasso minimizes squared error plus a penalty on the sum of absolute weights:

minimise  Σᵢ (Γᵢ − Σⱼ wⱼ Rᵢⱼ)²  +  λ · Σⱼ |wⱼ|

Larger λ → more weights forced to zero → fewer rules. You pick λ by cross-validation (LassoCV, which this project uses).

new concept

The instability problem

Candidate rules are highly correlated (rule "cd4 ≤ 300" and "cd4 ≤ 280" overlap heavily). When several columns explain the data almost equally well, Lasso picks one somewhat arbitrarily — change a few rows and it may choose a different one. The selected rule list can swing wildly under tiny data perturbations. For a clinical tool, that's unacceptable: the explanation must be reproducible.

Pause & check. If you reran Lasso on a slightly different sample and got a completely different rule list, would you trust it to guide a treatment decision?
No — and that's the motivation for the next section. We want rules that survive resampling, not rules that happen to win today's tie.
Section 10 · new concept

Stage 2 (real): stability selection — let the data vote

The idea, in one sentence: if a rule is real, it will be selected again and again across many resampled training sets; if it's an artifact, it will come and go. This is stability selection (Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2010).

The procedure

  1. Split the known patients into train (70%) and calibration (30%). Calibration is set aside untouched for the error bars in Section 11.
  2. Fit LassoCV once on train to fix a penalty λ*. (One CV, not one per bootstrap — saves compute.)
  3. Repeat B times (default 500): draw a bootstrap sample of the training rows with replacement, fit Lasso(λ*), and record which rules get nonzero weight.
  4. For each rule j, stability πⱼ = fraction of the B runs that selected it.
  5. Keep rules with πⱼ ≥ 0.7; order by πⱼ; enforce at most 4 conditions per rule; cap the list at 10.

Tiny worked vote (B = 5)

R2 = (cd4≤300 & age>50)  selected in runs 1,2,3,4,5 → π = 1.00  KEEP
R1 = (cd4≤300)           selected in runs 1,3       → π = 0.40  drop
R3 = (cd4>300)           selected in run  2         → π = 0.20  drop

R2 is a robust signal; R1 and R3 are unstable. The threshold 0.7 means "elected in at least 70% of votes."

you know this

This is bootstrapping — you've met it in random forests

A random forest already draws bootstrap samples for each tree. Stability selection draws bootstrap samples and runs a selection step on each. Same resampling primitive, different purpose: forests average predictions, stability selection counts selections.

A subtle bug this project fixed. Bootstrap draws are with replacement: patient 3 might be drawn twice in one sample. An earlier implementation collapsed each draw to a boolean mask, which silently converted with-replacement sampling into without-replacement — so duplicates stopped counting twice and the stability scores were biased. The fix passes per-point counts as sample_weight to Lasso: 
draws    = rng.choice(n, n, replace=True)
weights  = np.bincount(draws, minlength=n)   # patient drawn twice → weight 2
lasso.fit(R, Γ, sample_weight=weights)
Small implementation detail, large statistical consequence.
Why this also controls false positives. Under exchangeability, stability selection bounds the expected number of spurious rules roughly by q² / (π_thr − 0.5), where q is the average number of rules selected per bootstrap. So the 0.7 threshold isn't arbitrary — it buys a form of false-discovery control.
Section 11 · new concept

Stage 3: conformal intervals — honest error bars with a guarantee

We have a short rule list. But each rule's effect estimate could be off. Clinicians need to know by how much. A regular confidence interval assumes a model; we want one that makes almost no assumptions. That's conformal prediction.

The recipe, using the left-out 30%

  1. For rule j, the point estimate is the average pseudo-outcome of calibration patients satisfying the rule: τ̂ⱼ = mean(Γ over rule j).
  2. For each calibration patient in the rule, measure how far they are: sᵢ = |Γᵢ − τ̂ⱼ|. These are nonconformity scores — big means the rule's single number represents that patient poorly.
  3. Take q = the 90th percentile of those scores.
  4. The interval is [τ̂ⱼ − q, τ̂ⱼ + q].

Because the calibration set was held out at random, the next patient is exchangeable with it, which yields the guarantee: the true effect falls in the interval at least 90% of the time. No normality, no model assumptions.

Worked example: rule R2 on calibration {ids 3,4,10,11}

Γ  = [0.30, 0.10, 0.25, 0.15]
τ̂  = mean = 0.20
s  = [0.10, 0.10, 0.05, 0.05]
q  = 90th percentile of s ≈ 0.10
interval = [0.20 − 0.10, 0.20 + 0.10] = [0.10, 0.30]

The entire interval is above zero → we can confidently recommend treating this subgroup.

Reading the decision

IntervalInterpretationRecommendation
entirely > 0benefit almost surelyTreat
entirely < 0harm almost surelyAvoid
straddles 0too uncertain to callMore data / suggest with caution
new concept

Censoring adjustment: weighted quantile

The calibration set still contains dropouts among its neighbors. Each patient gets an IPCW weight so that under-represented survivors count more. Instead of a plain percentile, we take a weighted percentile of the nonconformity scores (sort, cumulate weights, find where the cumulative weight crosses 90%). This keeps the guarantee valid under censoring. In code: _compute_ipcw_weights and _weighted_quantile.

Pause & check. Why do we compute q on a held-out set rather than on the training data?
Training patients influenced the rule itself, so their residuals are optimistically small. Held-out scores preserve exchangeability, which is the assumption the coverage guarantee rests on.
Section 12 · engineering details

Handling small subgroups without fooling yourself

Conformal intervals are only as good as the calibration subgroup. If a rule matches 3 patients, the 90th percentile of 3 scores is one lucky/unlucky patient away from nonsense. Two safeguards are on by default.

1. Minimum support

Drop any rule with fewer than min_calib_support = 20 calibration patients. Tiny subgroups are exactly the ones that produce extreme, unstable CATEs with enormous intervals — they help no one. (On very small real datasets this is lowered, e.g. to 5; with our 9-patient toy you'd lower it to ~3 just to see anything.)

2. Shrinkage toward the global mean

For a rule with n calibration patients, blend its mean with the overall calibration mean:

w        = n / (n + k)            # weight toward the global mean
τ̂_shrunk = (1 − w)·mean(rule) + w·global_mean

The constant k is chosen so that a rule at the minimum support gets pulled by exactly shrinkage = 0.5. Effect: a well-supported rule (large n) keeps its own estimate; a borderline rule is pulled halfway to the average, preventing one patient from creating a "miracle drug for a handful of people" claim. This is the same bias-variance trade you saw in ridge regression, applied to subgroup means.

Honesty note. Both stabilizers trade a little bias for a lot of stability. The project reports them explicitly rather than hiding them — a habit worth copying in any applied ML work.
Section 13

Putting it together: predicting for a new patient

Suppose a new patient walks in with features x. Where do they land?

  1. Evaluate each kept rule in order of decreasing stability.
  2. The first rule that matches assigns the patient that subgroup's CATE and interval (this is a rule list, like a decision list — order matters).
  3. If no rule matches, fall back to the default: the overall calibration mean and its interval.

The output per patient is exactly four things: the CATE, its 90% interval, the matching rule, and a treat/avoid/caution recommendation. In code: _apply_rule_list and model.predict(X, return_details=True).

Pause & check. Two rules both match a patient: a very stable one and a less stable one. Which applies, and why is that sensible?
The more stable rule, because rules are checked in stability order. The most reliably discovered subgroup gets first claim on the patient; later rules only catch those the earlier ones miss.
Section 14

How we grade all of this (and why it's tricky)

For normal ML, you compare predictions to held-out labels. Here the label we care about — the true CATE — is never recorded in real data. We can't check it directly.

The semi-synthetic trick

Keep real patient features and real treatment assignment, but simulate the survival outcome from a known formula. Now we know the ground-truth effect for every patient, so we can grade every method exactly. The project does this for four simulation scenarios (data-generating processes):

  • AFT-Gumbel — simple additive effect on log survival time.
  • Cox PH — proportional hazards; effect is constant over time.
  • Non-PH crossing — the treatment effect reverses sign at some time; a hard case.
  • Nonlinear XOR — effect depends on an interaction of features, so no single feature predicts it.

Each runs on four datasets (PBC, SUPPORT, GBSG, ACTG175), with identical train/test splits for every method, repeated several times to see variance. In code: python/fair_benchmark.py and python/experiments.py.

Four different scorecards

No single number tells the whole story, because the metrics answer different questions:

MetricQuestion it answers
MAE / RMSEHow wrong is the estimated size of the effect?
Spearman rankDoes it rank patients correctly — who to treat first?
Rule countIs the output short enough for a human to read?
CoverageDo the 90% intervals actually contain the truth ~90%+ of the time?

Statistical significance is checked with paired tests and Holm correction for multiple comparisons (a way to avoid "we ran 20 tests and one looked good" false discoveries). Both an easy and a hard effect-size regime are reported, because small effects can push every method's R² below zero regardless of true quality.

Section 15

Glossary and the pitfalls worth remembering

Glossary

TermMeaning
CATEPersonalized treatment effect for a patient type; the thing we estimate.
CensoringOutcome unknown because the patient left before the horizon.
Horizon t*The fixed time we ask "alive by then?".
IPCWRe-weighting so censored/dropout patients don't bias estimates.
Propensity e(x)P(treated | features); corrects confounding.
Pseudo-outcome ΓA per-patient synthetic target whose expectation is the true effect.
Stability selectionSelect features by how often bootstrap runs choose them.
Conformal intervalDistribution-free prediction interval with a finite-sample coverage guarantee.

Five pitfalls this project ran into

  1. Wrong label construction. Setting Y = Δ instead of Y = (T > t*) flips dead patients to survivors. Always test your target against a hand-worked case.
  2. Bootstrap without multiplicities. Collapsing with-replacement draws to a boolean mask breaks the bootstrap. Use per-point counts as weights.
  3. Unstable selection. A single Lasso on correlated features gives different answers on different days. Vote instead.
  4. Tiny subgroups. A rule matching 3 people can produce a spectacular, meaningless effect. Require minimum support and shrink.
  5. Testing on the training rows. Conformal validity needs held-out calibration data; reuse breaks the coverage guarantee.

The one-paragraph summary. Turn survival data into a binary outcome at a horizon, keep only knowable patients and reweight with IPCW, build a per-patient pseudo-effect with the DR-learner so ordinary regression becomes possible, grow random forests on that target and harvest their split paths as candidate rules, select a short stable rule list by bootstrap voting instead of one fragile Lasso, then attach a conformal 90% interval to each surviving rule so every recommendation comes with an honest, assumption-light error bar. That is CISCaRL.
